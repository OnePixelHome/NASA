NASA data and resources

Every NASA source used by Status: GO, what the simulator does with it, and the exact quote it relies on, checked against the original page or PDF. In the simulator, each number carries the tag shown here (for example NASA S6), so players can trace it back. This list is generated from the simulator's own source file.

NASA S1NASA Earth Fact Sheet (radius, GM, solar irradiance)

Earth's radius and gravitational parameter behind every orbit formula: period, eclipse, coverage, transfers.

NASA S2NASA CSLI — CubeSat 101 (budget reserve, ground stations, deployable solar panels)

The budget rule: keep at least 10% in reserve. The budget meter turns amber and the budget score drops when a design eats into it. It also backs two lock reasons: a downlink too small for the data, and fixed body panels that cannot power the spacecraft.

“When possible, project reserves of at least 10% should be part of the budget to address any unexpected events.” — Chapter 2, p. 13 (page 21 of the PDF)
“Launching with an inadequate ground station is a mission killer.” — Chapter 2.10, p. 22 (page 30 of the PDF)
“deployable solar panels will nearly double the power input to the spacecraft” — Contents, p. iv (page 6 of the PDF), ChargerSat-1 caption

NASA S3NASA — Design and Develop Science Missions (Launch, Space, Ground, User segments)

The four mission segments, used as the four team roles: Science, Spacecraft, Ground and Launch.

NASA S6NASA — Landsat 9 mission (705 km, 98.2°, 185 km swath, 16-day revisit, 10 years of consumables)

The Sun-synchronous orbit card and the Multispectral Imager (30 m pixels, 185 km swath). The model reproduces its ~99-minute orbit and ~16-day revisit. Its 10 years of consumables set the 10-year mission in this orbit.

“Consumables: 10 years” — Mission page, quick facts
“Design Life: 5 years” — Mission page, quick facts (the design life is shorter)

NASA S9NASA Small Spacecraft Technology State-of-the-Art — Communications (high data rates call for X-band and a high-gain antenna)

Why the Data downlink tier offers fast radio links to ground stations: a mission with a lot of data picks a high-frequency link. Shown when a downlink can carry the data. The data rates themselves stay illustrative values.

“a mission with high data rate needs would select a high frequency such as X-band for downlink and a directional high-gain antenna” — Section 9.2.4, Design Considerations
“NASA spacecraft, which use the government bands of S-band, X-band, and Ka-band, may use the NASA Near Space Network (NSN).” — Section 9.2.1, Frequency Bands

NASA S10NASA — International Space Station FAQ (51.6° inclination)

The 51.6° inclination of the Low Earth Orbit card, which is why it never flies over the poles.

NASA S12NASA Earth Observatory — Blue Marble: Next Generation (August 2004, with topography and bathymetry)

The picture of the Earth in the mission film and the first image the spacecraft takes. Credit: NASA Earth Observatory.

NASA S13NASA JPL — GRACE-FO Launch Press Kit (490 km orbit, five-year prime mission)

The 5-year mission in Low Earth Orbit: a real NASA mission at almost the same altitude (490 km) was planned for five years.

“Prime mission: Five years” — Quick Facts, Mission (page 9 of the PDF)
“Orbit altitude: Approximately 305 miles (490 kilometers)” — Quick Facts, Mission (page 9 of the PDF)

NASA S15NASA Glenn and JPL — Performance and Comparison of Lithium-Ion Batteries Under Low-Earth-Orbit Mission Profiles (NASA/TM-2007-214826)

How deeply the battery may be drained on every lap in low orbits. The game allows 30%, inside the range NASA tested for low Earth orbit, where 40% is the outer limit.

“A 40 percent depth-of-discharge (DOD) was chosen for this testing in order to test the outer envelope of possible DODs for Li-ion batteries for LEO applications.” — Page 11 of the PDF

NASA S16NASA Aerospace Battery Workshop 2018 — GOES-R Lithium-Ion Battery Life Test (Lockheed Martin, NASA Goddard, Saft)

How deeply the battery may be drained in geostationary orbit. The game allows 70%, below the 75% of the longest eclipses in the GOES-R life test. Eclipses there come once a day in two seasons a year, so the battery cycles far less than in a low orbit.

“Maximum eclipses at 75% DOD” — Conclusions (slide 16)
“Eclipse season is 44 days; Eclipse discharge once per day” — Life Test Description (slide 9)

NASA S17NASA and ISRO — NISAR mission quick facts (imaging radar at 747 km)

Where real imaging radars fly. It supports the 700 km reference altitude of the Imaging Radar; the 1,000 km limit itself stays an illustrative value.

“747 km” — Quick facts, “Orbit Altitude”

NASA S19NASA Space Place — Launch a rocket from a spinning planet (Earth turns at 1,675 km/h at the equator)

The extra 0.4 km/s a Sun-synchronous launch costs. 1,675 km/h is 0.47 km/s; at the 28.5° latitude of the launch site that is 0.41 km/s of eastward push, which a launch towards the pole cannot use.

“At the equator, Earth's surface is rotating at 1675 kilometers per hour (1041 miles per hour)!” — Launch windows article

Partner agencies

Two values come from NASA's partners. NISAR, above, is a joint NASA and ISRO mission.

NOAA S14NOAA and NASA — GOES-R Series spacecraft (15 years of on-orbit life in geostationary orbit)

The 15-year mission in Geostationary Orbit, the on-orbit life required of the GOES-R weather satellites.

“Spacecraft on-orbit life of 15 years with orbit east-west and north-south position maintained to within +/-0.1 degree” — Satellite Driving Requirements

ESA S18ESA — Sentinel-1 facts and figures (imaging radar at 693 km)

A second imaging radar, from the European Space Agency, flying at almost the same altitude as NISAR.

“Orbit: Polar, Sun-synchronous at an altitude of 693 km” — Facts and figures
